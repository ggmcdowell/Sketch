# Chapter 1: The Last Run
*Battle Aces: End of Peace*

**Mercury - Launch Station Orbis - 2650**

---

Lathe Anderson awoke before his alarm, a phantom vibration still echoing in his skull from the MERIT implant—years of programmed routine etched into neural pathways. The single room housing unit in Mercury Harbor Station offered little comfort – just a narrow bunk, a basic sanitation unit, and the dull, persistent hum of the station's life support. He hadn't bothered with the light; darkness was cheaper, and he preferred it. It matched the feeling of being perpetually underground, a sentiment that clung to everyone who called Mercury home.

He was already dressed in the dark brown coveralls he'd slept in, practicality winning out over comfort. His duffle bag was packed and waiting by the door – a standard-issue container holding the few personal belongings he allowed himself. No room for sentimentality when every kilo counted against payload weight. He pulled on his worn mag boots, the familiar click of the magnetic soles engaging a small comfort. The metal was cold against his feet, a constant reminder of the unforgiving environment outside the station's protective shell.

He ate a ration bar in the dark, the synthetic protein nougat offering little in the way of flavor. Metallic, bland, efficient. It was fuel, nothing more. He chewed slowly, deliberately. He scanned his compad while eating, the pale light illuminating his face. Mostly station notices, shipping manifests, and automated system updates. In the corner of the screen, his MERIT status blinked green: *Flight hours: 8,247 | Fuel efficiency: 94th percentile | Next certification: 3 runs*. Then, a message from his father.

A wave of something akin to guilt washed over him. He'd been putting it off for weeks. His father, a decorated freighter pilot himself, always asking about his progress, always subtly hinting at his absence. Lathe knew his father meant well, but the messages felt like a weight, a reminder of the life he *should* want. A life that required… connection. He scrolled through the message, his thumb hovering over the reply function.

*"Son, just checking in. How are the runs going? Your mother and I were talking about you. We haven't seen you in months. Come visit us in the Ice Bowl when you have a moment. It's… peaceful there. A good place to recharge."*

The Ice Bowl—a bleak, desolate region on the northern pole of Mercury where the temperature rarely rose above freezing. A place where families and retired miners lived to stay out of the way of industry, close to the water harvesting. A place Lathe actively avoided. Not because it was sad – it was – but because it demanded time, demanded *presence*. Time he didn't have, presence he couldn't afford. He marked the message as read, intending to reply later. Much later.

He finished the ration bar, the synthetic aftertaste lingering on his tongue. He left the housing unit, stepping into the cold, metallic corridor. As always, the station was bustling with activity, workers hurrying to meet quota. Another freighter would rotate into his unit soon, another anonymous face passing through. He didn't bother acknowledging the few nods he received. He had a run to make.

As he had read earlier on his compad, today's freight run was a standard haul of iron from Mercury to Venus, a vital component in the construction of all things. After all the technology and all the progress, humanity still needed the building blocks of structure: Iron. Routine, predictable, profitable.

He walked the cold hallways leading to the main lift, the rhythmic thrum of the station's machinery a constant companion. He avoided small talk with the other workers on the lift, offering only curt nods in response to their greetings. He preferred to conserve his energy, to focus on the task at hand.

An advert played on a small screen above the door, barely hanging on, its loose bracket just one bolt away from collapse. A small speaker elsewhere echoed the importance of the construction armada while glorified images displayed the true power of Iron and Sons. Cities erected, stations deployed—but more importantly and recently—the great Solar Catch: an ever-expanding array built close to the sun. The promise of unlimited free energy for all. The advert continued by highlighting the endless workshops and foundries the armada boasted.

"Apply today and build something for the ages," it invited.

Lathe had tried.

As the lift ascended, he reflected on the blue-collar atmosphere of Iron and Sons, the industrious spirit that permeated every level of the organization. The workers here weren't dreamers or idealists. They were pragmatists, driven by necessity and a relentless work ethic. He saw a family saying goodbye to a father leaving for the armada, the wife's forced smile betraying her fear. The children clung to his legs, their faces etched with a mixture of pride and sadness.

He had considered starting a family himself, once. But the life of a freighter pilot wasn't conducive to domesticity. Months spent in transit, weeks in cryo-sleep, the constant risk of accidents or pirate attacks. It wasn't a life he could impose on anyone.

He had three runs left until he could re-apply to join the armada fleet, a prospect that both excited and terrified him. The armada offered stability, prestige, a chance to contribute to something larger than himself. But it also meant relinquishing the freedom he'd come to cherish, the solitude of the long haul.

His turn came up at the central logistics desk, and he stepped forward, exchanging a brief nod with Mon Rag, the clerk he'd known for years.

"Lathe, good to see you. Still hauling iron, I see." Mon's voice was gruff, but a hint of amusement played on his lips. "Heard you're almost armada-eligible. About time, eh?"

"Just trying to earn my keep, Mon," Lathe replied, offering a curt smile.

Mon chuckled. "Speaking of keeping busy, your sister still single? You never introduced me..."

Lathe cut him with a look.

"Whoa, a man has to try," Mon backpedaled. "Take it easy. You know females are rare as hen's teeth on Mercury."

Lathe had seven brothers and one sister, youngest of the bunch. With Mercury's heavy metals and proximity to the sun, the odds of having a daughter were about one in twenty-seven—a fact that made his sister something of a rarity.

Lathe lowered his shoulders. "Maybe next time, Mon," he lied.

Mon took the hint and returned to filling out the digital paperwork, noting Lathe's fuel resupply and payload. "Long Walk still holding together, I see. That old bucket of bolts has a new repair every time I see it."

"She's reliable, Mon. And faster than she looks." Lathe defended his ship, a familiar pride swelling within him.

"There are newer ships, Lathe. With better AI, more efficient engines. You could afford one, you know. Stop throwing credits away."

"They're not faster, Mon. And my AI is well-tuned. She understands me. We work together." Lathe's voice was firm, his loyalty to the *Long Walk* unwavering.

Mon sighed. "Suit yourself. But don't come crying to me when a pirate jumps you out on the road because you're missing sensors." He paused, then asked, "Route?"

"J21."

Mon raised an eyebrow. "J21? That's… unconventional. A lot of dead space out there. But less traffic, I'll give you that."

"It's faster," Lathe stated, his tone leaving no room for argument.

"Faster, or just… reckless?" Mon warned, his expression serious. "There's been pirate activity near the Venus approach. You'll be flying right through it."

Lathe took his credentials and nav data on a hard drive, ignoring the warning. He headed to the flight deck, donning a lightweight rental flight suit and helmet that smelled like disinfectant. He didn't bother securing the helmet. He preferred to feel the air on his face and avoid the smell.

He rode another open lift up to the primary dock, his eyes scanning the exterior of his ship as he approached. The *Long Walk* was a patchwork of repairs and modifications, a testament to his ingenuity and resourcefulness. When docked vertically it measured almost forty stories tall, as big as they come. The long cargo forks stretched into the depths of the station. Between them, countless bulk containers filled with refined iron ore sat securely fastened by mechanical braces and brackets.

It was his father's ship, handed down from brother to brother. By the time he got it, it was already worn from the harsh elements of space. One long score in particular was his father's favorite story, a close call with a derelict shipping platform in the asteroid belt.

As the lift reached the top, the ship revealed its small pilot section and a thin walkway leading to the entry door. He approached the door and paused.

"Irene, it's me. Open up," he said. The dock attendant on the lift raised an eyebrow.

A mechanical lock chunked away and the door slid open. The familiar scent of recycled air and oil greeted him. He stepped into the cramped cockpit, the pilot's chair worn smooth from decades of use. His father's chair. His chair now.

The screens flickered to life as Irene initialized the preflight sequence.

```
╔════════════════════════════════════════╗
║ LONG WALK - PREFLIGHT STATUS           ║
║                                        ║
║ CARGO: 8,247 tons (iron ore)           ║
║ FUEL: 94% (sufficient for J21)         ║
║ LIFE SUPPORT: Nominal                  ║
║ REACTOR: Online, 3% output             ║
║ RCS FUEL: 87%                          ║
║                                        ║
║ ROUTE: J21 (Venus direct)              ║
║ ESTIMATED TRANSIT: 47 days             ║
║ CRYO SCHEDULED: Hour 6                 ║
║                                        ║
║ PILOT: Lathe Anderson                  ║
║ Flight Hours: 8,247                    ║
║ Status: Certified (Renewal: 3 runs)    ║
║                                        ║
║ MERIT SYNC: Active                     ║
║ Neural integration: 94%                ║
║                                        ║
║ ALL SYSTEMS GREEN                      ║
╚════════════════════════════════════════╝
```

"Everything looks good, Irene," Lathe said, settling into the chair. The MERIT implant synced with the ship's systems, a faint tingle at the base of his skull as neural pathways connected to thruster controls, navigation, heat management. It was as natural as breathing. He'd been flying with MERIT since he was sixteen—fourteen years of the implant learning his reflexes, optimizing his decisions, becoming part of him.

"Dock control reports ready for departure," Irene's voice was calm, feminine, familiar. "Cargo braces releasing in thirty seconds."

He pulled the harness over his chest, the magnetic locks clicking into place. Through the narrow viewport, he could see the station's dock crane beginning to retract. He looked up to the ceiling of the station and watched as the mighty crane moved into position.

He took a deep breath of planet air.

Just three more runs, and then his life could really begin.

The clamps released with a series of mechanical thuds that resonated through the ship's frame. Irene fired the RCS thrusters—short, controlled bursts that pushed the *Long Walk* away from the dock. The station began to recede, Mercury's dark surface visible beyond, pockmarked with mining scars and industrial complexes.

"Course to J21 plotted," Irene reported. "Main burn in five minutes. Recommend pre-cryo checklist at T-minus six hours."

Lathe nodded, though Irene didn't need to see it. His hands moved across the controls with practiced efficiency—fuel flow, heat radiators, cargo integrity. The MERIT implant fed him data in the corner of his vision: delta-v budget, optimal burn angles, fuel consumption projections. It wasn't intrusive. It was just… there. Like knowing where your hands were in the dark.

"Acknowledged," he said. "Let's get this done."

The main engine ignited, a low rumble that built into a steady roar. Acceleration pressed him into the seat—one point two Gs, standard for loaded freight. Mercury fell away behind them, the sun a blazing point of light off the port side.

Routine, predictable, profitable.

He watched the navigation display as they settled into the long burn toward Venus. Forty-seven days. Most of it in cryo-sleep, his body frozen while Irene monitored the ship, adjusted course, kept him alive. He'd done it hundreds of times before.

The MERIT implant hummed quietly at the edge of his awareness. Flight hours ticking up. Experience logged. Performance metrics optimized.

Three more runs.

He leaned back in the chair and closed his eyes, letting the ship's acceleration anchor him. The hum of the reactor, the whisper of air recyclers, the faint vibration of the main engine—these were the sounds of home. Not the Ice Bowl, not Mercury Harbor Station.

Here. In the dark. Between worlds.

Just three more runs.
